How to Get Your Electrical Contracting Business Ready to Sell

November 16, 2026 • 6 min read • By Carter Henderson

I have sat on the buyer's side of the table for a lot of owner-operated service businesses. The pattern is the same almost every time. The company is good. The customers are loyal and the crews are solid. But the books are a mess, and the buyer lowers the price to cover what they cannot see.

You can fix that before you ever talk to a buyer. It takes about a year of steady work, not a scramble in the last month. Here is the checklist I would follow for an electrical contractor.

Why the finance side decides your price

A buyer is paying for future profit. The only evidence of future profit is your past numbers. If those numbers are late, do not match the bank, or change every time someone asks, the buyer assumes the worst. Clean books do not make a weak business strong. They let a strong business get paid for what it is.

12 to 9 months out: get the basics right

  • Close the books every month. Pick a date, such as the 15th, and have last month finished by then. Every bank and credit card account matched to its statement.
  • Separate personal costs. Stop running family vehicles, phones and trips through the company where you can. For what stays, keep a simple list with receipts. A buyer will add those costs back to your profit, but only if you can prove them.
  • Make the books match the tax return. Buyers and their lenders compare the two. Work with your CPA so the differences are small and explained.

9 to 6 months out: show where the money is made

  • Track profit by type of work. Service calls, residential projects and commercial projects earn very different margins. Set up your accounting so each job carries its own labor, materials and subcontractor costs. A buyer will ask which work makes the money. You should know before they do.
  • Build a work-in-progress schedule. If you run jobs that last longer than a month, list every open job with its contract amount, cost to date, billing to date and estimated cost to finish. This shows whether you have billed ahead of the work or behind it. Buyers and banks ask for this schedule early.
  • Report your backlog. Signed work that has not started is one of the best things you can show a buyer. Keep a running list with expected start dates.

6 to 3 months out: clean up cash and risk

  • Collect what you are owed. Go through every receivable older than 60 days. Chase retainage on finished jobs. Old receivables make a buyer question both your customers and your billing.
  • Know your customer mix. List your top ten customers and what share of sales each one brings. If one general contractor makes up a large part of your work, a buyer will see risk. You may not be able to change it in six months, but you can show the history and the contracts behind it.
  • Sort out the license. In many states the company operates under a license held by a specific person, often the owner. Rules differ by state, so check yours. If the license leaves when you do, plan now for who will hold it after a sale.
  • Review your trucks and tools. Keep a current list with age and condition. A buyer will price in anything that needs replacing soon.

3 months out: build the package

By now the hard work is done. Pull it together so a buyer can get answers in days, not weeks:

  1. Three years of financial statements and tax returns
  2. Monthly statements for the last 24 months
  3. Your list of personal and one-time costs, with proof
  4. Profit by type of work
  5. Work-in-progress schedule and backlog
  6. Receivables aging and customer list by share of sales
  7. Equipment list, leases, and license details

The mistakes I see most

  • Starting too late. One clean month does not undo three messy years. Buyers want a track record.
  • Cutting costs to boost profit right before a sale. Buyers spot it. They will add the cost back in and trust the rest of your numbers less.
  • The owner as the whole business. If you are the estimator, the license holder and the main customer contact, the buyer is buying a job. Hand off what you can and document the rest.

Common questions

How long does it take to get ready?

Plan on 12 months if your books need work. If you already close every month and track job costs, you may need far less.

Do I need audited financials?

Most sales of smaller contractors do not require an audit. Buyers do expect statements they can tie to your bank records and tax returns.

Is this worth doing if I am not sure I will sell?

Yes. Everything on this list also makes the business easier to run. You will know which work makes money, collect faster, and see problems sooner.
